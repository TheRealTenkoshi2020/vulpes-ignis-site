Bleeding During or After Sex: What to Record and When to Get Help

What is useful to notice, and when should assessment happen sooner?

Source-checked education with original examples and practical questions.

For adults 18+. Education, not a diagnosis or personalized care.

Bleeding during or after sex can have several explanations. It is often not caused by a serious condition, but that is not a reason to repeatedly dismiss it. NHS guidance recommends having bleeding after sex or between periods assessed. [1]

This guide focuses on vaginal bleeding. If the blood is coming from urine, the rectum or a skin injury instead, tell the service; the assessment needs to match the source.

Notice the pattern without delaying care

Record when bleeding happened, how much there was, whether it stopped, and whether it has happened before. Mention pain, discharge, a missed period, possible pregnancy, contraception, menopause-related changes, medicines and any recent examination or procedure.

You do not need an exact measurement. “A little spotting on two occasions” and “bleeding that is continuing and making me feel faint” communicate different levels of concern. If you cannot identify where the blood is coming from, say that rather than guessing.

A note should make the appointment easier. It should not become a reason to postpone assessment until you have collected more examples.

Keep possible causes separate from the diagnosis

NHS guidance lists potential contributors such as cervical changes, polyps, dryness and infections. The list explains why different assessments may be appropriate; it does not let a person identify the cause from the timing alone. [1]

Avoid assuming that the explanation is necessarily friction, a partner's technique or an STI. Someone can have a symptom requiring care without anyone having done something wrong. Equally, an injury or unwanted activity deserves its own attention rather than being explained away as ordinary spotting.

Recognize combinations that need faster help

A missed period, unusual bleeding and abdominal or pelvic pain require urgent assessment because an ectopic pregnancy is one possible explanation. Severe pain, heavy bleeding, fainting or feeling acutely unwell warrants emergency care. Do not use this article or a home pregnancy test to rule out an urgent problem. [1]

If pregnancy is known or possible, tell the service immediately so that it can advise the appropriate route. If bleeding follows a significant injury, say what happened and seek prompt help.

For a medical emergency in the US, call 911 or use emergency care. You do not need to establish the cause before asking for urgent assessment.

Bleeding after menopause needs its own evaluation

NHS guidance recommends assessment of vaginal bleeding after menopause even when it is a small amount or has happened only once. Many causes are treatable and not serious, but the purpose of evaluation is to identify what is happening, including conditions that should not be missed. [2]

Tell the clinician about hormone treatment and the bleeding pattern. Do not assume that a medicine or an earlier episode provides a permanent explanation for every later change. Ask what is expected for your treatment and which changes should prompt contact.

Ask what the appointment may involve

The clinician may discuss the history, pregnancy testing, STI testing and an examination. Further assessment can include imaging or other procedures depending on what is found. You can ask what each step is intended to answer, whether there are alternatives and what consent or comfort choices are available. [1] [2]

An examination should be explained. Tell the clinician if pain, trauma history, disability, sensory needs or other concerns affect what would make the visit manageable. You can ask to pause rather than assuming that agreeing to attend means agreeing to every proposed step.

While arranging care, stop contact that causes pain or further bleeding. Another form of closeness, or a pause altogether, can be a freely chosen response. The useful goal is accurate assessment and comfort, not completing an activity despite a warning sign.
