Before Trying to Conceive: A Sexual-Health and Preconception Checkup

What deserves a conversation before we start trying?

Source-checked education with original examples and practical questions.

For adults 18+. Education, not a diagnosis or personalized care.

Planning a pregnancy can begin with a conversation, not a shopping cart full of supplements. A preconception visit is a chance to discuss health, medicines, infection prevention and reproductive goals before decisions become urgent.

It can also be useful when pregnancy is a possibility rather than an immediate plan. You do not need to promise a timetable, a particular family structure or certainty about parenthood to ask good questions.

Put your actual goals on the agenda

Explain whether you are considering pregnancy soon, exploring future options or trying to understand how a health condition affects the decision. Include the practical conditions that matter: care access, support, finances, work demands, disability accommodations or uncertainty about the plan.

An original starting sentence is: “I would like to understand what deserves attention before a pregnancy and what can wait.” That invites priorities instead of an unmanageable list of everything that could possibly go wrong.

If another person is involved in the reproductive plan, discuss how information and responsibilities will be shared without making one person responsible for everyone's health behavior.

Bring the complete medication and supplement list

CDC recommends discussing medical conditions and all medicines, including prescriptions, over-the-counter products, vitamins and supplements, before pregnancy. The exact product and dose help the clinician assess safety and timing. [1]

Do not stop a medicine independently because a pregnancy is being considered. Some treatments need adjustment, while leaving a condition untreated may also create risk. Ask what changes, if any, are needed, who will coordinate them and when to review the plan.

Include nonprescription products marketed for hormones, fertility, relaxation or sexual performance. “Natural” does not tell the care team what the ingredients do or whether they suit a possible pregnancy.

Review prevention before it becomes an urgent question

Vaccination history, infection screening and sexual-health concerns can be part of the visit. Bring available records and describe relevant activities rather than assuming an old “full panel” covered everything. A clinician can identify which tests or vaccines are appropriate and whether timing affects pregnancy planning. [1]

Some people also need discussion of HIV prevention, previous STIs, reproductive procedures or a partner's health history. These are practical care questions, not tests of whether someone is sufficiently responsible to become a parent.

Agree on how new results will be communicated and what follow-up is necessary before or during a pregnancy.

Ask about folic acid and everyday support

CDC recommends 400 micrograms of folic acid daily before and during early pregnancy to help prevent certain brain and spine birth defects. Some medical histories require individualized advice, so a larger dose should not be assumed to be better. Ask how the recommendation fits your situation and existing products. [1]

Discuss nutrition, alcohol, tobacco and other substances without trying to design a perfect lifestyle first. A useful conversation identifies realistic support and the most important next step. Financial or energy constraints deserve a plan that compensates for them, rather than instructions that assume unlimited time and money.

Include family history and fertility questions

Tell the clinician about relevant inherited conditions, previous pregnancy losses, reproductive concerns or treatment that may affect fertility. They can explain whether genetic counseling or specialist assessment would be useful. A referral is a way to answer a particular question, not a prediction that something will go wrong. [1]

If conception requires donor sperm, donor eggs or another supported pathway, ask about planning and assessment early. You do not need to follow a calendar intended for a different reproductive situation. ASRM's fertility guidance recognizes that evaluation should fit the circumstances involved. [2]

Leave with a short priority list

This original Vulpes Ignis appointment sheet has four sections: decisions needed before trying; tasks that can happen alongside planning; people responsible for follow-up; and the date to review unanswered questions.

Include one relationship question if useful: “How do we keep affection and sexual choice from becoming only a conception project?” Planning for a pregnancy can support health without turning either person into a system that must perform on command.
