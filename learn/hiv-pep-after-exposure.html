HIV PEP: Getting Help After a Possible Exposure

Possible HIV exposure? Seek urgent assessment now. PEP should start as soon as possible, ideally within 24 hours and no later than 72 hours after exposure.

For adults 18+. Read to learn and prepare questions; a qualified professional can help with your own care.

If a recent exposure could involve HIV, seek urgent medical assessment now. PEP should start as soon as possible, ideally within 24 hours and no later than 72 hours after exposure. Do not wait for symptoms, a partner's test or the end of this article. [1]

PEP means post-exposure prophylaxis: a short course of HIV medicines used after a potentially significant exposure. It is a time-sensitive healthcare option, not a judgment about how the exposure happened.

Explain the event without needing perfect words

Start with one sentence: “I may have been exposed to HIV at about this time, and I need a PEP assessment.” An emergency department, urgent-care clinician or sexual-health service may be able to help. If a service cannot provide it, ask where you can receive assessment immediately.

Useful information includes when the event happened, which body sites and fluids were involved, what barriers were used, any information about the other person's HIV treatment or viral suppression, and whether you already use PrEP. Unknown information can stay unknown; do not delay care while trying to reconstruct every detail. CDC recommends case-by-case assessment when the source's status is unknown. [1]

An exposure can occur during consensual activity, a barrier failure, injection-related contact or an assault. You deserve care in each situation.

Understand what the first assessment does

The service will consider whether the exposure warrants PEP and assess your health and HIV status. A baseline HIV test cannot tell you whether infection resulted from an exposure that just occurred. Its purpose is part of deciding on appropriate care now, with follow-up testing later. [2]

When PEP is indicated, CDC says the first dose should not be delayed while laboratory results are pending. Tell the clinician about medicines, supplements, allergies, kidney or liver concerns, possible pregnancy and any recent HIV prevention medicines. These details help them choose a suitable regimen; they are not reasons to decide on your own that help is unavailable. [2]

If prescribed, make the whole course accessible

NIH describes the usual PEP course as 28 days. Taking it as prescribed and completing follow-up are important parts of the plan. PEP is not intended to replace an ongoing prevention strategy for repeated exposures. [3]

Before leaving, ask whether you have the complete course or a starter supply, where the remaining medicine will come from, and what to do if the pharmacy cannot fill it. Discuss side effects and who can help if they become difficult. Do not stop, share, double or substitute medicines without advice from the prescribing service.

If cost or insurance is a problem, say so immediately: “I need a plan I can actually obtain today.” A prescription that cannot be accessed is a problem the service needs to know about.

Put follow-up dates somewhere useful

CDC's updated guidance includes follow-up contact and HIV testing after the initial assessment. The exact plan depends on the situation and recent prevention medicines. Ask for dates, the tests required and a contact for questions rather than relying on an online calendar alone. [2]

Also ask about testing or prevention for other infections and pregnancy-related care where relevant. HIV PEP does not provide comprehensive STI or pregnancy protection. New symptoms deserve contact with the care team even when a follow-up appointment is already scheduled.

Ask about what happens afterward

If HIV prevention will remain relevant, discuss transition to PrEP. CDC describes a clinician-guided transition after PEP without an unnecessary gap. The medicines, testing and follow-up still need to fit your circumstances. [1]

If more than 72 hours have passed, contact a healthcare service anyway. They can consider testing, ongoing prevention and other care needs. Do not translate “time-sensitive” into “there is no point seeking help now.”

A short care-seeking note

This original Vulpes Ignis note can travel with you: the approximate exposure time; relevant contact; current medicines; how to obtain the prescription; the next appointment; and the service's contact details. Keep it only somewhere that is safe for your circumstances.

You do not need to settle blame, disclosure or a relationship decision before getting an urgent assessment. The immediate task is access to care.

ONE SMALL THING TO TRY

Seek urgent assessment first. If PEP is prescribed, ask how to get the whole course, what follow-up you need and whom to contact if taking it becomes difficult.
