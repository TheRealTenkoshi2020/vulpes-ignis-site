Ask a Coach: What if I don't know my boundaries yet?

Uncertainty is a valid starting point; you can begin with the choices you know.

Educational Q&A: an illustrative editorial question, not a reader consultation or professional coaching session.

For adults 18+. Education, not a diagnosis or personalized care.

You do not need every answer before setting a limit

You do not need a complete list of preferences to deserve respect. Some boundaries are clear immediately, while others become clearer only after you have time to consider a situation. The Hotline emphasizes that consent must be freely given and can be withdrawn. An uncertain answer is not a provisional yes, and another person's impatience does not create an obligation to decide.

A person can say 'I don't know yet' or 'I'm not ready to talk about that.' Those statements are meaningful information about the present. If a partner responds by bargaining, guilt, or ridicule, the concern is not that your wording was insufficient. The ability to decline or postpone a decision is part of genuine choice.

Begin with conditions that help you feel comfortable

Rather than listing every imaginable activity, consider what conditions help you feel able to choose freely. You might value privacy, time to think, a clear explanation, being able to leave, or someone accepting a no without punishment. These are examples of personal preferences, not a standardized psychological assessment. Different people may want different conditions.

Try writing 'I feel comfortable making decisions when…' and completing the sentence privately. You can leave it unfinished and revisit it later. A boundary does not have to be supported by a dramatic past experience to count. You can decide what you are comfortable with today even if you do not know how you will feel next month.

Understand what a boundary can and cannot do

A boundary describes your choices and how you intend to respond when a situation is not acceptable. It might be 'I do not want photos taken,' or 'If I am pressured to discuss this, I will leave the conversation.' It is different from telling another person which friends they may have or requiring access to their private messages. Healthy relationships involve respect for both people's autonomy.

love is respect describes boundaries as ways of communicating expectations and recognizing what each person needs. You can discuss mutual agreements, but an agreement does not make it acceptable for someone to threaten or control another person. A boundary should help clarify participation rather than become a disguise for coercion.

Supporting sources: [2]

Practice uncertainty without apologizing for it

You may be accustomed to softening a refusal so that other people will not feel disappointed. But clear, respectful language does not need a lengthy defense. 'Not now,' 'I'm not sure,' and 'Please don't ask again today' can communicate present limits. The Hotline states that consent requires an informed, freely given decision; people cannot provide that decision when they feel unable to decline.

If the other person needs information about their own plans, they can make a choice based on your current uncertainty. They are not entitled to force a deadline for intimate decisions. Likewise, you can acknowledge another person's preference without changing your own. Being considerate does not require surrendering control over your answer.

Supporting sources: [1]

Changing a boundary is not evidence you were dishonest

People may learn about themselves, gain new information, or experience changing health and relationship circumstances. A boundary may become stricter or more flexible over time. The Hotline emphasizes that consent can be withdrawn and must apply to the specific activity at hand. A prior yes does not mean future permission has been permanently granted.

If you want to revise a boundary, name the new decision without pretending the earlier one was invalid. A partner can ask what the new agreement means but should not shame you for changing your mind. Likewise, reconsidering one activity does not change every other limit. Specific decisions deserve specific conversations.

Supporting sources: [1]

Notice the reaction to a clear no

A respectful person may be disappointed but will still stop asking about an activity you have declined. Repeated pressure, humiliation, threatening to leave as punishment, or trying to isolate you from people you trust are concerns that deserve attention. The Hotline distinguishes informed, willing choices from decisions extracted through fear or manipulation.

If you feel unsafe, focus on practical safety and confidential support rather than repeating an explanation. No phrase can reliably make someone respect a limit they have decided to ignore. You are not required to keep negotiating or to disclose personal experiences in order to be believed. The right to stop the conversation is itself meaningful.

Supporting sources: [1] [2]

An exercise that fits on one page

Write three headings: 'Easy yes,' 'Not today,' and 'Still thinking.' Choose only a few non-explicit examples such as meeting in public, texting at certain times, sharing photographs, or discussing sensitive topics. This is a Vulpes Ignis journaling prompt, not a formal consent tool or clinical assessment. Do not treat any entry as binding permission.

Read the list privately and notice whether it gives you clearer language. You can share parts with someone you trust, keep it to yourself, or discard it entirely. A useful boundary does not need to be permanent to be valid now. What matters is being able to make decisions without pressure and having those decisions respected.
