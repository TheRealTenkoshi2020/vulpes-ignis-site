After Sexual Assault: Medical Options, Support and Your Choices

What care and support can I consider, even if I am unsure about reporting?

Source-checked education with original examples and practical questions.

For adults 18+. Education, not a diagnosis or personalized care.

You can seek care after sexual assault even if you are unsure what happened, have not decided about reporting, or do not want to tell the whole story. Care can address injuries, time-sensitive prevention, forensic options and support.

If there is immediate danger, serious injury or another emergency, seek emergency help. Possible HIV exposure needs urgent assessment for PEP as soon as possible and no later than 72 hours. If pregnancy is possible, emergency contraception may be available within five days; the suitable option and timing need prompt advice. [1] [2]

Begin with the care you want now

You can say: “I need medical care after an unwanted sexual experience. Please explain my options before each step.” A support person or specialist advocate may be helpful if you want one.

You do not need to decide every next step at once. Medical assessment, collecting forensic evidence and making a report are related possibilities, but they are not the same decision. RAINN explains the purpose and process of a sexual assault forensic examination and ways to find a suitable service. [3]

Ask the service which choices are available locally and which decisions are time-sensitive. Local procedures and legal requirements vary.

Ask promptly about HIV and pregnancy prevention

HIV PEP is an emergency prevention course for certain exposures, not a medicine everyone automatically needs. A clinician evaluates the exposure and relevant history. Do not wait for every detail to be known before seeking that assessment; CDC emphasizes starting eligible treatment promptly within the 72-hour window. [1]

Pregnancy-related care is a separate discussion. CDC describes emergency contraceptive pills and a copper IUD used within the relevant five-day window, with differences between options. Tell the clinician or pharmacist about timing and medicines so the recommendation fits your circumstances. [2]

Even if one prevention window has passed, injury care, testing, follow-up and support may still be useful. Do not interpret a missed window as losing the ability to seek care.

Discuss testing and treatment without self-prescribing

CDC's post-assault guidance includes assessment for relevant STIs, possible preventive treatment, vaccination considerations and follow-up. The plan depends on the person and exposure; it is not a reason to take leftover antibiotics. [4]

Ask what tests are being offered, which results will need follow-up and whether an early negative result could require later testing. Also ask about side effects and how to obtain medicines.

An STI result alone may not establish when an infection was acquired. CDC notes that an infection identified after an assault may have existed beforehand; medical care and legal interpretation are different questions. [4]

Understand forensic-exam choices

A forensic examination can document findings and collect evidence. RAINN explains that a survivor can ask questions and choose whether to participate in individual parts of the process. Ask about consent, support, evidence storage and reporting options at the service you contact. [3]

If you have washed, changed clothes or waited, you can still seek care and ask whether an examination could help. There is no single forensic deadline that this guide can safely apply to every location and circumstance. Do not delay medical attention to work out evidence procedures on your own.

Ask explicitly about any mandatory-reporting rules or confidentiality limits that apply before relying on an assurance about reporting.

Make the next appointment easier to reach

Before leaving, request a written plan if it is safe to keep: medicines, results, follow-up dates, urgent symptoms and a contact for questions. CDC emphasizes follow-up for results, further testing when appropriate, vaccination and care after preventive treatment. [4]

Tell staff if receiving a voicemail, portal notification or bill could create a privacy problem. Ask what the service can arrange. If transport, cost or returning to the same setting is difficult, name that barrier so it can be included in the plan.

Support does not require a particular reaction

You may feel distressed, numb, uncertain, angry or focused on practical tasks. This article cannot predict your response or define how recovery should look.

An original choices card can say: “Help I want today; questions I want answered; a person I want nearby; a safe way to contact me.” You can leave any line blank. RAINN provides survivor-support information alongside forensic-exam guidance. [3]

Your next step can be small and specific. Seeking information does not require promising what you will decide afterward.

ONE SMALL THING TO TRY

If useful, make one note for yourself: a short choices-and-support card that does not require retelling the experience.
