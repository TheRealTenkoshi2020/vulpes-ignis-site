Beyond Words: Gentle Ways to Check In

A signal works only when everyone agrees on its meaning.

For adults 18+. Education, not a diagnosis or personalized care.

On this page · 7 sections

People may struggle to find words in a vulnerable moment. Pre-agreed signals can make it easier to request a pause, more space, or a change of pace.

Try it together

Outside an intimate moment, choose a few simple signals: a raised hand for pause, a spoken word for stop, or a gesture meaning 'please ask me.' Practice responding calmly to each one.

An everyday example

During a cuddle, one partner raises a hand. The other immediately gives space and asks whether to continue later.

Consent and comfort

Nonverbal signals supplement, not replace, affirmative consent. Silence, freezing, or lack of resistance is not agreement. If a signal is unclear, stop and check in verbally. Neither partner owes an explanation for declining or changing their mind.

A conversation starter

What would make it easiest for you to ask for a pause without feeling awkward?

Make it your own

Choose one part of the idea that fits your relationship and leave the rest. If you try it, check in afterward: did this feel enjoyable, neutral, or uncomfortable? Curiosity is useful even when the answer is 'not for us.' Respect privacy, accessibility needs, and differences in desire.

Putting the idea into practice

Agree on signals before you need them

A pause signal is useful only when both people know what it means and what happens next. Practice a simple response: stop, create space, and ask a short question when the other person is ready. Do not invent elaborate codes that are hard to remember during stress.

When silence is not a yes

Someone who becomes quiet, still, or withdrawn may be overwhelmed. Do not assume a lack of protest means an activity is welcome. Stop and check in; if the person cannot communicate freely, do not continue. A signal system should make consent clearer, never replace it.

A deeper dive

Agree on meaning before the moment

A raised hand is useful only if both people know whether it means pause, stop, or ask a question. Choose a signal that remains visible in the environments where you might use it. A spoken stop word can be a backup, but it is never the only acceptable way to withdraw consent. If someone becomes quiet, stiff, withdrawn, or otherwise seems uncertain, stop and check rather than waiting for a perfectly executed signal.

Practice the response

Try a nonsexual rehearsal while planning a date: one person uses the pause signal and the other responds with 'Okay, we're stopping.' Then discuss what response felt respectful. Avoid joking about the signal or testing whether a partner will obey it. If either person cannot communicate clearly, postpone the activity. A shared signal is a convenience, not permission to ignore other signs of discomfort.
