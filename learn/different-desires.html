When you want different things

Desire doesn’t have to match perfectly for the conversation to matter.

For adults 18+. Education, not a diagnosis or personalized care.

Start with the difference itself

Two people can care about each other and want different amounts or kinds of intimacy. Treat the difference as something to understand, rather than evidence that one person is broken or the other is unreasonable.

Ask what each of you means by closeness. One person may be thinking about sex, another about touch, uninterrupted conversation, or feeling wanted. Defining the words can reveal where the actual difference lives.

Take obligation out of the negotiation

No one owes sexual activity to maintain a relationship. Disappointment can be real without becoming pressure. A useful conversation allows both “I miss this” and “I don’t want this now” to be heard.

“I’d like to understand what connection feels good to each of us. Can we talk without deciding we have to do anything tonight?”

Ask about the conditions

What makes connection easier? What makes it feel like work? What kinds of closeness are welcome right now? These questions can help you find shared ground without demanding identical preferences.

You can make a menu together: things both people welcome, things that need a fresh conversation, and things outside the boundary. A menu is a conversation aid, never a standing invitation.

Know when more support would help

If a change in desire worries you, is persistent, or appears alongside pain or a health change, you can discuss it with a qualified clinician. For recurring relationship conflict, a suitably qualified counselor may offer structure. Getting support need not mean deciding who is at fault.

ONE SMALL THING TO TRY

Ask, “What is one kind of closeness you would enjoy this week, with no expectation that it leads anywhere else?”
