Asexual and Aromantic Lives: Closeness Without a Required Script

Can relationships and intimacy fit me if sexual or romantic attraction works differently?

Read at your own pace. Use the questions and examples that fit your life.

For adults 18+. Read to learn and prepare questions; a qualified professional can help with your own care.

A fulfilling adult life does not have one compulsory relationship shape. Some people experience little or no sexual attraction. Some experience little or no romantic attraction. Others experience either kind of attraction only under particular conditions or at particular times.

You can explore that language without needing to prove a label, date someone, avoid dating, or resolve every question today. The purpose of vocabulary is to help you describe your life, not to create another rulebook.

Separate attraction, behavior and preference

Sexual attraction, romantic attraction, sexual behavior and feelings about particular activities are related questions, but they are not interchangeable. Stonewall's glossary distinguishes ace- and aro-spectrum terms and recognizes that people may use more than one word to describe their experience. [1]

A person may identify as asexual and want a romantic relationship. An aromantic person may or may not want sexual contact. Someone may welcome affection while avoiding certain kinds of touch. Rather than inferring an entire relationship plan from one label, ask what the person means and wants.

You can also decide that none of the available words fits. An imperfect label does not invalidate a clearly stated preference.

An identity is not a symptom to repair

AASECT's professional position states that asexuality and ace-spectrum identities are not disorders requiring correction. It opposes attempts to change those identities through conversion or reparative approaches. That is a position about dignity and competent care, not an instruction to assume that every person's experiences are identical. [2]

An ace or aro person can still seek care for pain, a new unwanted change, medication effects or another concern. Conversely, a clinician should not assume that a person's orientation is evidence of a hormonal problem. State the question you want answered: “I am comfortable with my orientation; I want help with this symptom.”

The same respect applies in relationships. “You have not met the right person” is an assumption about someone else's experience, not a helpful plan.

Describe closeness in concrete terms

Instead of beginning with what a relationship is supposed to contain, name the forms of connection that interest you. These might include companionship, shared routines, romance, affectionate touch, sexual contact, mutual support or living independently.

The list can include “not wanted,” “uncertain” and “not something I want to discuss.” You do not need to assign each preference a permanent meaning. Nor does one person's interest create an obligation for someone else to participate.

For example: “I would like a committed partnership and separate sleeping space,” or “I enjoy affectionate touch, but I do not want it to imply sexual contact.” Those original examples describe possibilities, not recommended relationship models.

Discuss differences without turning them into a debt

Partners can have different needs or preferences while treating each other respectfully. Start with accurate information: What is wanted? What is freely available? What is outside someone's boundaries? What assumptions have been left unspoken?

A compromise about scheduling or shared activities can be freely chosen. Sexual contact accepted because someone feels indebted, afraid or responsible for preventing a partner's distress is a different concern. A relationship agreement should leave room for an actual no.

Sometimes compatible arrangements are available; sometimes an important difference remains. Recognizing that difference is more honest than promising a change in attraction to keep a relationship intact. Support can help people make decisions without assigning either person the role of defective partner.

Disclosure belongs to you

You can share a label, explain only a boundary, or decline a conversation. If disclosure could affect housing, finances, work or personal safety, consider the circumstances before treating openness as a compulsory milestone.

A short explanation may be enough: “I use this word because it helps describe my attraction. What matters for our relationship is that I do and do not want these things.” You can correct a misunderstanding without offering your entire personal history.

An optional closeness menu

Make three columns: “I welcome,” “I might discuss,” and “I do not want.” Add activities, relationship expectations and privacy preferences rather than scoring your sexuality. Keep the page private if that suits you, and revise it when useful.

There is no required result. The point is to make more room for an adult life that reflects your actual choices.

ONE SMALL THING TO TRY

Describe a kind of closeness you value, or a boundary you want understood. Use a label if it helps you explain yourself; you can also describe your experience in your own words.
