HIV PrEP: Ongoing Prevention That Fits Your Life

What would starting and maintaining PrEP involve?

Source-checked education with original examples and practical questions.

For adults 18+. Education, not a diagnosis or personalized care.

PrEP is medicine taken by a person without HIV to reduce the chance of acquiring HIV. It is an ongoing prevention option, distinct from PEP, which is assessed after a potentially significant recent exposure. Choosing to ask about PrEP is a healthcare decision, not a statement about someone's character or relationship. [1]

If an exposure may already have happened, tell the clinician when it occurred. Do not assume that starting a routine PrEP plan is a substitute for urgent PEP assessment.

Start with the prevention goal

Describe the activities and circumstances you want help planning around. That might include a new partner, uncertainty about a partner's HIV status, a relationship with different HIV statuses, changing barrier use or another relevant exposure.

Identity alone does not answer the clinical question. Nor do you have to wait for a relationship crisis to ask. CDC encourages discussion of PrEP as part of HIV prevention and explains that it does not prevent pregnancy or other STIs. Barriers, vaccination and testing can still have separate roles. [1]

Be direct about what would make a plan workable: fewer daily tasks, predictable appointments, medication interactions, cost, privacy or transport. Practical fit matters because protection depends on using the chosen medicine as prescribed.

Know that options have expanded

PrEP is available in oral and long-acting injectable forms. HIV.gov describes approved injectable options including cabotegravir and lenacapavir; they have different schedules and starting procedures. This is why an older article listing only one kind of injection may not describe the full current choice. [2]

Ask which options are appropriate for the exposure route and your health circumstances. Not every oral formulation has the same indication, and a convenient schedule does not remove the need for follow-up. Your clinician should explain the initial steps, when protection is expected and what to do about late or missed doses or appointments.

Do not borrow a friend's schedule, especially an intermittent schedule found online. Evidence and recommendations differ by medicine and exposure route.

Expect testing before and during care

HIV testing is required before PrEP, and testing continues during follow-up. The choice of tests can depend on recent PrEP or PEP use and possible symptoms of recent HIV infection. Starting prevention in someone who already has HIV requires a different clinical approach. [3]

Other assessment depends on the medicine. For example, kidney function matters for oral tenofovir-based options, and hepatitis B needs consideration when starting or stopping certain oral PrEP medicines. Bring your health history and medication list rather than deciding that one test result makes every option suitable. [3]

Ask which tests are part of your plan, how results will be delivered, and who you should contact if a new exposure or symptom occurs between appointments.

Make starting, continuing and stopping one conversation

A useful prescription conversation covers more than the first dose. Ask about expected side effects, which effects warrant contact, how refills work, and what happens if insurance or transport changes.

Long-acting medicines also need a plan for delayed injections or stopping. Drug remaining in the body does not mean reliable protection continues indefinitely; the provider needs to explain follow-up and alternative prevention where relevant. [2]

Do not discontinue an existing medication without discussing the implications. If the plan becomes hard to maintain, contact the service early. A practical adjustment is more useful than disappearing from follow-up because you feel embarrassed about a missed visit.

Bring five questions to the first appointment

This original Vulpes Ignis checklist keeps the decision grounded:

  1. Which available options fit my activities and medical history?
  2. What testing and appointments would each require?
  3. When would protection begin, and what should I use in the meantime?
  4. What should I do after a missed dose, a late appointment or a possible exposure?
  5. What will medicine, visits and laboratory work cost, and how will billing and messages be handled?

You can take time to choose. The useful outcome is an understandable prevention plan with support you can actually access.

ONE SMALL THING TO TRY

If useful, make one note for yourself: a first-appointment checklist covering goals, medicines, testing, cost and follow-up.
