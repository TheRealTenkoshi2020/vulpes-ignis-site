How to build a yes, maybe, no list

A simple framework for discussing preferences without negotiation pressure.

For adults 18+. Education, not a diagnosis or personalized care.

A list helps you talk; it does not decide for you

A yes/maybe/no list is a simple way to organize questions about preferences and boundaries. It can include forms of affection, relationship expectations, privacy choices, or ideas about consensual adult exploration. It is not a medical or psychological test, and it does not predict compatibility. Its value is giving people a starting point for a conversation that might otherwise feel difficult.

A mark on a list does not authorize anyone to act. The Hotline explains that consent is ongoing, specific to an activity, and open to withdrawal. A 'yes' written last week is not permission for today. A 'maybe' is not a softened yes. A no means the person has not agreed, even when the idea matters to their partner.

Supporting sources: [1]

Decide what your three columns mean

Words can carry different meanings for different people, so define your categories before sharing them. One possible approach is: yes means 'I'm comfortable discussing this or might want it in the right circumstances,' maybe means 'I'm uncertain and need more information,' and no means 'Do not propose this to me as an activity.' Those are editorial definitions, not universal standards.

Another person may prefer a separate 'not now' or 'I don't want to discuss this' category. Blank entries should also be permitted. The list should create freedom to name uncertainty rather than pressure to fill every box. You can revise it at any time, and you are not obliged to explain every decision or historical experience that informs a boundary.

Supporting sources: [1] [2]

Make the first list privately

People sometimes agree too quickly when someone they care about is looking over their shoulder. Completing a list alone can provide a quieter opportunity to notice which topics interest you and which feel uncomfortable. Choose ordinary, non-explicit examples to begin: hugging, discussing intimate topics, sharing photos, maintaining separate social accounts, or planning affectionate time together.

Notice the difference between 'This interests me as a fantasy' and 'I might choose this in real life.' Those statements describe different kinds of curiosity. You can add a comment such as 'Only with a longer conversation' without moving an idea into yes. A list is allowed to be incomplete. It is a record of what you know now, not a promise about who you must become.

Supporting sources: [1] [3]

Share only what you choose

If you want to compare lists with another consenting adult, first ask whether they want the conversation. Some people welcome this kind of reflection, while others would rather discuss one question at a time. Either preference can be respected. Do not insist that someone share a completed list, and do not photograph or forward their responses without permission.

Decide what privacy means before exchanging information: Is the list temporary or saved? Who can see it? Can either person request that a copy be deleted? Healthy relationships, according to The Hotline, include honesty alongside a right to personal space and privacy. A private response is not automatically evidence of dishonesty.

Supporting sources: [3]

How to compare lists without bargaining

Focus on mutual interest rather than trying to convert a partner's answer. If one person says yes and the other says no, there is no shared agreement for the proposed activity. If someone says maybe, ask whether they want information or would rather leave the subject alone. The aim is to find areas that both people willingly discuss, not to create a queue of tasks that a partner must eventually complete.

RAINN advises asking and checking in rather than assuming permission. You might say, 'We don't have to decide anything tonight,' or 'I see this isn't for you; I won't keep bringing it up.' Those sentences are Vulpes Ignis examples. The important condition is that someone can decline without punishment or an exhausting debate.

Supporting sources: [1] [4]

Risk questions belong before agreement

Some activities carry physical, emotional, or privacy risks that a preference list cannot explain. The National Coalition for Sexual Freedom's consent guidance calls for explicit prior permission and discussion of foreseeable risks and boundaries before kink activities. A checkbox does not provide the specialized knowledge or informed discussion a high-risk activity may require.

Do not use the list as an instruction manual for unfamiliar or dangerous behavior. If a topic raises questions about physical safety, health, legal rules, or capacity to consent, leave it undecided until appropriate information is available. You can also decide never to try it. The presence of an idea on a list is not evidence that it would be safe or lawful.

Supporting sources: [5]

A list is not a substitute for a stop signal

Prior discussion cannot guarantee that someone will feel the same way later. NCSF emphasizes the ability to withdraw consent during an activity and recommends an agreed way to communicate stopping. You may use ordinary words such as 'stop' or 'no'; no person should be forced to remember special terminology before being taken seriously.

If someone becomes distressed, reluctant, impaired, or unable to communicate freely, do not continue based on an old list. Discuss what a pause means before any mutually chosen activity, but accept that consent always depends on the person's present choice. A boundary is meaningful only when others respond to it.

Supporting sources: [1] [5]

What if your answers don't match?

Different preferences do not mean one person is defective or the relationship has failed. Some differences can be accommodated by choosing activities both people enjoy; others may represent limits that cannot be compromised without pressure. love is respect notes that people sometimes discover they are not on the same page about what they want, and that can be a valid reason to reconsider a relationship.

Ask what each person needs from the relationship instead of treating the no column as a problem to solve. A respectful outcome might be continued conversation, a different shared activity, or recognition of an incompatibility. No person owes a change in their boundaries to demonstrate that they are sufficiently open-minded.

Supporting sources: [2]

A gentle example to begin with

Try choosing three everyday topics: affectionate touch, privacy around messages, and how to initiate a difficult conversation. For each topic, write whether discussing or engaging in it sounds welcome, uncertain, or unwelcome. Add a brief note only if you want to. Leave the page private until you decide otherwise.

If you later talk with a partner, begin with something like, 'I want to understand what feels comfortable to both of us, not make a plan we have to follow.' That sentence is a Vulpes Ignis suggestion, not a proven intervention. The most useful part of this exercise is the ability to make a choice and have it respected.

Supporting sources: [1] [3] [4]

Consent and capacity: further reading

Choose freely, check agreement as circumstances change, and stop when willingness is uncertain. Someone who is asleep, unconscious, or too impaired to understand and choose cannot give meaningful agreement. These are educational safety principles; legal definitions and age requirements vary by jurisdiction.
