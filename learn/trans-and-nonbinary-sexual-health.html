Sexual Health for Trans and Nonbinary Adults: Care Based on Your Body and Goals

How can screening, contraception and comfort discussions fit my actual needs?

Source-checked education with original examples and practical questions.

For adults 18+. Education, not a diagnosis or personalized care.

A useful sexual-health appointment should fit the person attending it. Your gender does not tell a clinician which organs you have, which activities you enjoy, whether pregnancy is possible or what help you want.

You deserve relevant questions, understandable explanations and language that lets you participate. This guide offers a way to prepare that conversation without requiring you to disclose your identity or history to a partner, employer or anyone outside your care needs.

Begin with the question you want answered

Write the concern in your own words: pain with a particular activity, an STI test, contraception, fertility, a change after hormones or surgery, or an examination you find difficult. You can request a private conversation before discussing sensitive details.

An original opening might be: “Please use these words for my body. I want to discuss testing today, and I would like you to explain why any examination is needed.” Another could be: “My records do not describe my current anatomy accurately. Can we correct the information relevant to this visit?”

You need not present a complete autobiography to ask for focused care.

Match STI screening to anatomy and exposure

UCSF's transgender-care guidance recommends a sexual history and testing informed by the person's anatomy and sexual practices. A gender marker alone cannot determine which sample sites are relevant. [1]

Ask whether your actual exposures call for urine or genital testing, throat or rectal swabs, blood tests, or another assessment. A test taken from one site does not automatically answer questions about every site. Tell the clinician about symptoms and recent exposure timing so the test and follow-up discussion fit the situation. [1]

Useful questions include: “What does this test check?”, “What does it miss?” and “Would an earlier test need repeating?” Prevention discussions can include barriers, vaccination and HIV prevention according to individual needs, rather than assumptions about identity. [1]

Keep pregnancy prevention explicit

If you have a uterus and ovaries and have sex that can introduce sperm into the vagina, ask whether pregnancy is possible in your circumstances. CDC states that testosterone might not prevent pregnancy and should not be used as contraception. It also advises discussion of contraception for people using testosterone who do not want pregnancy. [2]

The absence of bleeding is not a contraceptive method. Ask which options fit your preferences, health history and hormone regimen, and what to do after an unprotected exposure. Do not independently alter prescribed hormones to resolve a pregnancy concern; contact the relevant clinician. [2]

A future-parenthood conversation can be separate from a contraception conversation. Wanting one, both or neither is a valid starting point.

Describe comfort changes without dismissing them

If discomfort begins after treatment or surgery, bring the timing, location, triggering activity and associated symptoms to the treating team. Ask which changes are expected and which need assessment. UCSF emphasizes relevant anatomy and surgical history when planning sexual-health care. [1]

Avoid assuming that every symptom is caused by gender-affirming treatment. Equally, a clinician should not ignore a treatment history that could matter. Your question can be practical: “What possibilities are you considering, and what would help distinguish them?”

Do not push through pain or use an internet exercise as a substitute for an individualized postoperative plan.

Plan an examination you can participate in

Before an intimate examination, ask about its purpose, steps and alternatives. You can explain whether certain words, positions or unexpected touch make the process harder. Ask what can be adjusted, whether you can bring support, and how to signal a pause. Professional guidance emphasizes explanation, consent and attention to discomfort during intimate examinations. [3]

These requests are about access to care. You should not have to demonstrate comfort or confidence to deserve a respectful response. Discuss any limitations of an alternative test so that accommodation and clinical usefulness are considered together.

Make the follow-up usable

Before leaving, confirm the name and contact details the clinic should use, how results will arrive and who can see portal messages or billing information. Ask staff what their systems can and cannot keep private.

This original care-preferences note can travel with you if you want it to: relevant anatomy or treatment; preferred language; today's goal; examination preferences; safe result contact. Include only what is useful, and decide where it is safe to keep. The note supports your voice; it does not replace a conversation or informed consent.

ONE SMALL THING TO TRY

If useful, make one note for yourself: a confidential care-preferences and screening-questions note.
