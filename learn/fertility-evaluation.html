Trying Without a Scoreboard: When to Seek Fertility Evaluation

When would an evaluation be useful, and what does it involve?

Source-checked education with original examples and practical questions.

For adults 18+. Education, not a diagnosis or personalized care.

When pregnancy does not happen as expected, it can be tempting to turn every cycle, symptom and intimate moment into a report card. An evaluation offers a different task: identify which questions can be investigated and what choices are available.

You do not have to decide which partner is responsible before seeking help. Fertility can involve factors affecting eggs, sperm, the reproductive tract, timing or more than one person. Sometimes the available evaluation does not identify a clear explanation.

Use a timeline that fits the circumstances

ASRM's 2026 committee opinion generally recommends evaluation after 12 months of regular unprotected intercourse when the person supplying eggs is under 35, or after six months at 35 or older, when there is no known reason to investigate sooner. Over 40, more immediate evaluation may be appropriate. [1]

Those are not instructions to wait despite a concern. Irregular or absent periods, known or suspected endometriosis, previous relevant surgery or treatment, or possible sperm-related difficulties can justify earlier assessment. People needing donor gametes or another supported reproductive pathway also need advice tailored to that situation. [1]

Ask your clinician which timeline applies rather than forcing your circumstances into a generic definition.

Expect an assessment, not a single fertility score

A first visit commonly reviews reproductive and medical history, previous pregnancies, medicines, menstrual patterns and relevant symptoms. The clinician decides which examination and tests can answer the questions raised. Bring existing results so that unnecessary repetition can be discussed. [2]

The investigation may consider ovulation, reproductive anatomy or sperm. When a partner providing sperm is involved, their evaluation should not automatically be postponed until every other possible test has been completed.

Ask what each test is intended to establish, what it cannot predict, and how a result would change the next decision. “More testing” is not automatically the same as a more useful plan.

Understand the purpose of semen testing

ASRM describes semen analysis as an essential part of an infertility evaluation when relevant. It provides information about sperm number, movement and shape. A history of having conceived before does not remove the need to consider current sperm factors. [2]

Follow collection instructions and ask whether a repeat sample is needed. A result should be interpreted in the wider clinical context, not treated as a measure of sexual performance, masculinity or a person's worth.

The same principle applies to other laboratory results: ask what the measurement means for this decision rather than comparing isolated numbers online.

Keep consent in the conversation about timing

Information about a fertile window can support a plan, but a predicted date does not create an obligation for sexual contact. Discuss how each person wants to handle timing and what happens when someone is tired, in pain or unwilling.

An original example is: “I want us to understand the advice, and I also want an agreement that we can say no without blame.” If the conception process is placing strain on the relationship or emotional wellbeing, that impact deserves support alongside the medical assessment.

You can preserve forms of affection or shared time that are not evaluated for their reproductive usefulness.

Ask about costs and decisions before committing

Request an explanation of the proposed sequence, expected costs, insurance questions and alternatives. Ask which investigations are necessary now, which can wait, and what would trigger a referral or a different approach.

A fertility consultation does not require agreement to every available treatment. You can ask about success estimates, uncertainties, burdens and stopping points. A clinician should help explain the options in relation to your circumstances and goals.

Prepare a shared questions sheet

This original Vulpes Ignis note records the duration and circumstances of trying, relevant history, existing results, medicines, symptoms, each person's main concern and questions about cost or access.

End with: “What is the next useful step, and when do we review it?” That gives you a task that can be completed without asking one intimate encounter or one laboratory number to explain an entire future.

ONE SMALL THING TO TRY

If useful, make one note for yourself: a shared appointment-preparation sheet with questions for the clinician.
