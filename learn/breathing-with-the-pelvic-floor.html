Learning to Let Go: Breathing With the Pelvic Floor

An optional pelvic-floor awareness practice that emphasizes gentle breathing, release, and individualized assessment.

Breathing, relaxation and yoga

For adults 18+. Education, not a diagnosis or personalized care.

Pelvic-floor care can involve learning to release tension as well as learning to contract muscles. If every article you have seen begins with squeezing harder, there is another part of the conversation worth having.

The Royal United Hospitals Bath physiotherapy leaflet explains that a tense or non-relaxing pelvic floor may have difficulty contracting effectively. It discusses relaxation and emphasizes that an individualized assessment and exercise program are often needed. [1] A website cannot determine whether your muscles are weak, tense, poorly coordinated, or affected by something else.

The short practice below is an original awareness exercise informed by that guidance. It is not a diagnosis or a treatment for sexual pain, and it does not require you to feel a dramatic movement.

Begin with a position you can leave easily

Choose supported sitting or a comfortable resting position. If you lie on your back, you may prefer bent knees and supported feet. Use cushions where they help, and do not choose a floor position that makes getting up difficult.

Keep your eyes open if you prefer. You can place a hand over your lower ribs to notice ordinary breathing, but touch is optional. Let the room remain familiar: notice the chair under you, the light, and the surface supporting your feet.

Before focusing inward, decide that stopping is allowed. The practice does not need to continue into intimacy or produce arousal. It can simply be a brief pause in your day.

An optional awareness sequence

Notice first. Take two or three normal breaths without changing them. Observe whether you are bracing your abdomen, gripping your buttocks, or drawing your shoulders upward. You do not need to correct everything you notice.

Allow an easy inhale. If comfortable, let the lower ribs expand a little. Imagine the area between your sitting bones becoming less guarded as you breathe in. Do not push downward or try to move air into your pelvis; air remains in the lungs.

Let the exhale happen. Release the breath without adding a deliberate pelvic squeeze. Allow the next inhale to arrive naturally. Avoid breath-holding, straining, or forceful bearing down.

Return to the room. After a few comfortable breaths, let go of the image and notice your surroundings again. Change position if you want to. The Bath resource uses breathing and pelvic-release imagery; the shorter sequence and wording here are a practical adaptation, not its full clinical program. [1]

If you cannot feel anything

You may not notice the pelvic floor at all. That does not establish that you are doing the practice incorrectly, nor does it diagnose a problem. You can keep the exercise at the level of comfortable breathing or leave it out entirely.

Avoid repeatedly checking by squeezing, pushing, or searching for a sensation. The aim is awareness without effort, and an unclear sensation is something you can discuss with a pelvic-health physiotherapist. You do not need to solve it through stronger contractions at home.

If body-focused attention is uncomfortable, shift your attention to a neutral sight or sound instead. This is an optional practice, and choosing a different focus is a reasonable way to use it.

Bring symptoms into the conversation

Pain during sex, ongoing pelvic discomfort, bladder or bowel symptoms, and difficulty with muscle exercises deserve assessment rather than a conclusion that you simply need to relax. You can ask: "Would pelvic-health physiotherapy be useful, and what should I practice or avoid while we investigate?"

Describe what you actually experience: the movement or activity associated with symptoms, whether symptoms persist afterward, and any exercises that make things worse. A short, accurate description is more useful than arriving with a fixed label such as "tight pelvic floor."

During intimacy, the practical lesson is permission to pause or change what you are doing. Breathing should never be used to coach someone through unwanted or painful activity. No amount of calm turns a boundary into an invitation to continue.

Learning about relaxation can expand your options. Finding out which options fit your body requires attention to your symptoms and, when needed, individualized care.

ONE SMALL THING TO TRY

If comfortable, notice a few ordinary breaths in a supported position. Avoid pushing down or forcing a pelvic contraction.
