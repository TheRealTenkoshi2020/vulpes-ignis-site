After a Positive Chlamydia or Gonorrhea Test: Treatment, Partner Care and Retesting

What happens after the result, beyond telling a partner?

Source-checked education with original examples and practical questions.

For adults 18+. Education, not a diagnosis or personalized care.

A positive STI result gives you a healthcare task. It does not supply a verdict about your worth, a reliable timeline of a relationship, or permission for anyone to treat you badly.

The useful next step is to understand exactly which infection and body site were identified, then leave with a treatment and follow-up plan. Chlamydia and gonorrhea have some similar care steps, but their treatments and testing instructions are not interchangeable.

Confirm what the result actually says

Ask which infection was found and whether the sample came from urine, the vagina, throat, rectum or another site. A result from one site does not automatically answer every question about another. Tell the clinician about symptoms, relevant contact, allergies, medicines and possible pregnancy. Those details can affect evaluation and treatment. [1] [2]

You can request the result in writing and ask what additional tests are appropriate. If the result is unexpected or confusing, ask the service to explain it. Avoid turning uncertainty into a private experiment with antibiotics.

Make the prescription practical

Follow the prescribed treatment, including the complete course where one is given. Ask how to take it, which interactions matter, what to do about a missed dose or vomiting, and which side effects require contact. The regimen should be chosen by the treating clinician, including for pregnancy or complicated infection. [1] [2]

Before leaving, check whether the medicine is available, affordable and obtainable that day. Tell the service if the pharmacy cannot fill it. Someone else's tablets or a partial leftover course are not a substitute.

If symptoms persist after treatment, contact the service rather than repeatedly restarting medication. Persistent symptoms can have several explanations, and the clinician may need further testing.

Ask when sexual activity can resume

CDC gives specific waiting instructions. For chlamydia, these include seven days after single-dose treatment or completion of a seven-day regimen, with symptoms resolved and partners treated. For gonorrhea, CDC advises avoiding sexual activity for seven days after treatment and until partners have been treated. Your clinician should explain how the instruction applies to the regimen and situation involved. [1] [2]

Do not assume that feeling better after one dose establishes clearance, or that using a condom changes every treatment-related instruction. Ask directly about relevant activities if the advice is unclear.

Partner care helps prevent repeat infection

Partners may need assessment and treatment even when they have no symptoms. Ask the service which partners should be contacted and whether it offers confidential partner-notification support. In some circumstances, services can provide partner-treatment options; availability and rules differ by jurisdiction. [1] [2]

An original conversation example is: “My test was positive for this infection. The clinic advised that you contact a service about testing and treatment. I can share the information they gave me.” You do not need to diagnose the partner or decide who was infected first.

If contacting someone could put you at risk, tell the clinician. A safer notification approach may be available.

Distinguish retesting from a test of cure

Retesting looks for a repeat infection later; a test of cure asks whether the original infection has cleared. CDC recommends retesting around three months after treatment for chlamydia or gonorrhea. A test of cure is needed in selected situations, including throat gonorrhea and chlamydia during pregnancy, with different timing. [1] [2]

Ask which kind of follow-up you need and book the date. Repeating some tests too early can complicate interpretation, so a self-chosen calendar is not always helpful.

Leave with one complete care note

This original Vulpes Ignis checklist records the infection and body site, medicine and instructions, sexual-activity guidance, partner-care plan, follow-up date and service contact.

Promptly report significant pelvic or testicular pain, fever, worsening symptoms or pregnancy-related concerns. The aim is treatment and support suited to the actual situation, without shame becoming another obstacle to care.
