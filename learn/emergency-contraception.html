Emergency Contraception: Options, Timing and What Comes Next

Need emergency contraception? Seek advice promptly. Options can be available up to five days after sex; method, timing and medicines affect the choice.

Read at your own pace. Use the questions and examples that fit your life.

For adults 18+. Read to learn and prepare questions; a qualified professional can help with your own care.

If sex could result in an unintended pregnancy and you want to prevent it, contact a pharmacist or healthcare service promptly. Emergency contraception works within time-limited windows, and the options differ. You do not need to be certain about your cycle before asking for advice. [1]

Emergency contraception is a backup prevention option after situations such as a barrier failure, missed contraception or sex without a pregnancy-prevention method. The next step is to identify the actual timing, available options and follow-up instructions.

Name the event and the current method

Tell the service when relevant sex occurred, whether there were several exposures, what contraception was being used and what went wrong. Bring the exact product names, any emergency contraception already taken, medicines or supplements, and information about possible pregnancy or breastfeeding.

Those details matter more than a general description such as “I missed the pill.” A pharmacist or clinician can help identify which advice applies. Do not spend a time-sensitive window searching for a single answer that is supposed to fit every product and person.

Understand the main choices

CDC's US guidance includes a copper IUD and emergency-contraception pills, including ulipristal acetate and levonorgestrel. A copper IUD can generally be placed within five days of the relevant exposure; clinicians can assess particular timing circumstances. Pills should be taken as soon as possible within five days. These are prevention options, not the same medicines used for abortion care. [1] [2]

Availability, medical suitability and the ability to obtain a procedure promptly can affect the choice. An IUD also raises a decision about ongoing contraception; someone can ask about emergency options without having already decided on a long-term method.

If a service discusses an option outside the methods described in this CDC page, ask what evidence and current professional guidance support it. Do not assume every type of IUD or pill has identical emergency use.

Timing and individual factors change the comparison

CDC reports that ulipristal has been more effective than levonorgestrel in the three-to-five-day interval after sex. Body weight and medicines can also affect the discussion; these questions should help someone find appropriate care, not make them assume that help is pointless. [1]

Ask: “Given the time, my current contraception and my medicines, which option can I realistically access now?” Do not independently increase a dose or combine products because of a social-media rule. If you vomit after taking a pill, contact a pharmacist or clinician promptly about the product-specific instructions.

Get a written plan for regular contraception

Restart instructions differ. CDC generally advises waiting at least five days after ulipristal before starting or resuming hormonal contraception, followed by the relevant backup protection. After levonorgestrel, regular contraception can generally be started or resumed immediately, with backup as advised. Clinician-administered methods can involve additional decisions. [1]

Ask for exact dates and the required backup method or period of avoiding pregnancy-risk sex. Emergency pills do not provide ongoing protection for later sex. Your plan should cover what happens after the dose, not only how to obtain it.

Arrange testing and know when symptoms need care

Ask when to take a pregnancy test and how to interpret bleeding changes. CDC advises a test if there is no withdrawal bleed within three weeks after emergency pills. If the result is positive or unclear, seek appropriate follow-up. [1]

Significant abdominal or pelvic pain, fainting or heavy bleeding needs prompt medical assessment, especially if pregnancy is possible. Emergency contraception does not answer separate STI or HIV-exposure questions; urgent PEP assessment may be relevant after some exposures. [3]

Keep the decision manageable

Keep the follow-up plan somewhere you can find it: exposure time; method and medicines; option chosen; exact restart and backup instructions; and pregnancy-test or follow-up date.

Using emergency contraception is a practical decision, not a failure of character. Once the immediate concern is handled, you can decide whether any change to the ongoing plan would make life easier.

ONE SMALL THING TO TRY

Seek advice promptly. Ask which option fits your timing and medicines, then get clear instructions for regular contraception, backup protection and any follow-up testing.
