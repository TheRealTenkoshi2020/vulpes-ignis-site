Ask a Coach: How do I tell someone about a positive STI result?

Lead with factual information, relevant boundaries, and respect.

Educational Q&A: an illustrative editorial question, not a reader consultation or professional coaching session.

For adults 18+. Education, not a diagnosis or personalized care.

Start from facts, not blame

Begin with an accurate understanding of what the test found. STI tests differ by infection and specimen, and a positive result should be discussed with the diagnosing clinician. The CDC recommends treatment and partner notification for certain infections, including gonorrhea and chlamydia, while specific instructions vary by diagnosis. The result does not identify someone's moral character or prove how they acquired the infection.

A clear disclosure may simply name the infection, the date you received the result, and the healthcare advice you are following. Avoid making medical promises you cannot verify. Someone else may need testing or treatment, but a general article cannot determine which person needs what without knowledge of the infection and exposure.

Learn the medical steps before advising others

Ask the clinician which medicine or follow-up is recommended, whether partners should be evaluated, when sexual activity can resume, and whether repeat testing is needed. For gonorrhea and chlamydia, CDC guidance includes recommendations about partner treatment and retesting. Other infections have different clinical pathways, and people should not assume that one timeline applies to every STI.

Do not tell a partner to take your prescription or use leftover medication. A qualified healthcare provider can determine appropriate care, including options for partner management where legally and clinically available. Knowing the actual plan makes the conversation more useful than giving someone an uncertain interpretation of a laboratory result.

Supporting sources: [1]

A brief disclosure can be respectful and specific

A possible script is 'My test was positive for [infection]. I'm following the clinic's instructions, and I wanted you to know so you can ask a healthcare professional about testing or treatment.' Replace the bracket with the actual diagnosis. This is an original Vulpes Ignis example, not a medical template or a guarantee that the conversation will be easy.

The other person may have questions or strong emotions. Share what you know, distinguish facts from uncertainty, and refer them to qualified guidance where necessary. It is reasonable to say 'I don't know yet' rather than guess. A disclosure is not an opportunity to assign blame when the time or source of an infection is unknown.

Supporting sources: [1]

Testing and treatment timelines depend on the infection

CDC recommends retesting three months after treatment for gonorrhea or chlamydia. That recommendation is about those infections; do not transfer it to every STI or treat it as the only follow-up a person might need. Ask the diagnosing clinician which tests and dates apply to you and whether any earlier assessment is necessary.

Ask when you and relevant partners may safely resume particular activities and which follow-up is important. Do not assume that feeling better proves treatment is complete or that an old negative result rules out another infection. If a symptom persists or worsens, contact the provider for appropriate evaluation rather than repeating an internet treatment plan.

Supporting sources: [1]

Safety may affect how a partner is notified

The CDC's partner-services guidance recognizes that notification can raise concerns about intimate-partner violence, privacy, and access to care. Some health departments or clinicians offer partner notification assistance, depending on the infection and available services. These options can be especially important when direct contact could lead to retaliation.

If disclosure might trigger threats or violence, ask a healthcare provider about safer methods and confidential support before contacting the person. Do not assume that an in-person conversation is always the best choice. Public-health needs and personal safety must both be taken seriously; a generic script cannot address every risk.

Supporting sources: [2]

Privacy matters even when notification is important

Ask the clinic which information is needed for care, reporting and partner notification, and who may receive it. CDC describes different partner-service arrangements and notes that practices and legal requirements vary. A notification conversation is not a reason to post another person's results or use a diagnosis publicly to settle a dispute.

Ask the provider how results are reported, whether partner services can help, and which information is necessary for follow-up. Avoid sharing someone else's diagnosis publicly as proof of a dispute. Accuracy and discretion can coexist. An STI result is a health event that deserves appropriate action, not an excuse to shame anyone.

Supporting sources: [2]

Prepare three questions for the clinician

Before a disclosure, ask 'What exactly was diagnosed?', 'What should my partner do?', and 'When is further testing or treatment needed?' If you are concerned about safety, add 'Are there confidential notification options?' These questions are a Vulpes Ignis care-note prompt, not individualized clinical guidance.

The conversation may be uncomfortable, but it can still be factual and respectful. The goal is to support timely care and informed decisions. Avoid promises about who transmitted the infection, how long it has been present, or whether everyone is now protected unless a qualified professional has supplied the necessary information.
