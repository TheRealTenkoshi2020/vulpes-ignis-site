Before sharing an intimate photo: consent and privacy

A digital image can travel much farther than a conversation.

For adults 18+. Read to learn and prepare questions; a qualified professional can help with your own care.

Permission needs a clear scope

An adult can choose to send a photograph to one person while declining any later sharing. Discuss screenshots, storage, forwarding and publication separately. The FTC describes intimate imagery distributed without consent as image-based abuse. The Hotline's consent guidance also distinguishes one agreed activity from permission for another; neither a relationship nor a previous photograph answers every new question.

Ask specific questions about who may receive a picture, whether it may be stored, and whether anyone else may see it. The other person can decline without needing to justify their privacy preference. Do not treat a relationship, a previous photograph, or a romantic conversation as standing permission to create or receive more intimate media. Adults should agree voluntarily before any such exchange.

Temporary messages do not guarantee disappearance

Some apps promise temporary viewing, disappearing messages, or encrypted transport. Those features can reduce certain risks but cannot establish that the recipient has made no copy. A different device, screenshot, notification preview, shared backup, or compromised account may preserve information after an app removes the visible message. Privacy depends on actual technical settings and human choices.

Review where media is stored and whether automatic backups, shared albums, or household accounts may have access. If losing control of a file would be unacceptable, choosing not to create or send it is a complete decision. The Hotline's internet-safety guidance recommends thinking carefully about what information digital services can reveal, especially when someone's safety depends on privacy.

Supporting sources: [3]

Adults only, without pressure or threats

Intimate images involving children require a separate child-safety response and must never be solicited, distributed, or handled as ordinary adult content. In adult relationships, a person should also never be pressured to provide material to prove love or honesty. Threatening to share images unless someone continues contact, pays money, or sends more material is a form of image-based abuse described by the FTC.

Someone experiencing threats does not owe the abuser compliance. Safety decisions can be complicated when monitoring or retaliation is possible, so confidential support may be useful. Avoid assuming that a direct confrontation, deleting an account, or changing passwords is always safe when another person may be monitoring devices. The priority is the affected person's wellbeing and ability to choose a response.

Supporting sources: [1] [3]

Synthetic images and altered content count as serious concerns

The FTC explains that image-based abuse includes manipulated material and intimate deepfakes, not only photographs taken in real life. A person can be harmed by a fabricated depiction even when they never sent an intimate photograph. The Take It Down Act establishes obligations for covered platforms concerning qualifying nonconsensual intimate visual depictions, with legal requirements and exceptions.

Do not assume that proof a picture is fake makes the harm irrelevant. Document abusive messages or postings when it is safe and lawful to do so, and seek trustworthy reporting support. Avoid forwarding the material to friends or posting it publicly as evidence, since that can increase circulation. The original image should not be uploaded to random services offering to remove it.

Supporting sources: [1] [4]

What the U.S. removal rules actually require

The Take It Down Act became law on May 19, 2025 (Public Law 119-12). The FTC says covered platforms must provide a removal-request process and remove qualifying nonconsensual intimate imagery and known identical copies within 48 hours after a valid request. Its enforcement notice identifies May 19, 2026 as the relevant platform-compliance deadline. Those requirements have a defined scope and do not establish that every website, image or request qualifies.

The FTC accepts reports about covered platforms that fail to remove material within 48 hours or lack a working removal process at TakeItDown.ftc.gov. Its consumer guidance also advises reporting perpetrators to local law enforcement and to the FBI at tips.fbi.gov. A person can also use relevant platform reporting processes and seek legal advice where appropriate. Report forms and legal remedies differ across jurisdictions. A general educational article cannot determine whether a specific incident qualifies for a civil claim or criminal investigation.

Supporting sources: [4] [1] [6]

An additional resource may help limit circulation

For adults who have a copy of the intimate image, the FTC points to StopNCII.org as a free resource for limiting further spread. Read its current eligibility, file-handling information and coverage before using it. A general guide cannot guarantee that a tool will cover every service or remove every copy.

The FTC gives different reporting resources for material involving minors, including NCMEC's Take It Down and CyberTipline. Follow official child-safety guidance rather than using an adult tool by default or forwarding the material. Be wary of anyone demanding original intimate files or payment while promising to erase every copy from the internet.

Supporting sources: [1]

Plan privacy rules before a relationship changes

People may feel comfortable sharing media at one stage of a relationship and want stricter limits later. Discuss how materials will be handled after a breakup, if a device is sold, or when a person asks for deletion. You can agree to honor deletion requests and never distribute media without permission, but no app can promise that every past copy has been destroyed.

A possible conversation is: 'I want us to be clear about whether anything can be saved or shared. I would prefer not to create images unless we both understand those boundaries.' These words are an original Vulpes Ignis suggestion, not a legal contract or proof of technical security. Choosing privacy is an ordinary expression of bodily autonomy.
