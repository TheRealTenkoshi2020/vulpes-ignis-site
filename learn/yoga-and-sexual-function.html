Yoga and Sexual Function: What One Small Trial Can Tell Us

Review a small yoga trial and a later low-certainty evidence review, then explore an adaptable chair-based session.

Breathing, relaxation and yoga

For adults 18+. Education, not a diagnosis or personalized care.

Yoga can be worth trying without claiming that a particular pose unlocks sexual function. Research offers some encouraging findings, but the size and design of the studies should remain visible beside the promise.

A 2013 randomized trial assigned 41 women aged 30 to 60 who had metabolic syndrome to a 12-week yoga program or a waiting-list control group. The yoga group showed greater improvement in the arousal and lubrication domains of a sexual-function questionnaire. [1] This is more specific than saying yoga improves every part of sex for every body.

The trial was small, involved a particular population, and used a waiting-list comparison. It could not fully separate the effects of yoga from factors such as attention, expectations, or participation in a structured activity. Its findings do not establish that a particular pose treats sexual dysfunction or changes hormones.

What later research adds

A 2024 systematic review of 10 randomized trials, involving 730 adults who were predominantly women, found a small improvement in sexual function compared with nonintervention controls. The authors rated the certainty of evidence as low and called for better, larger trials. [2]

That supports measured curiosity. Yoga may be a useful option for some people, while the evidence does not justify a guaranteed result or a universal prescription. These studies also do not validate the short chair-based sequence below.

Choose comfort before complexity

NCCIH recommends appropriate instruction and adaptations, especially for people with health conditions, injuries, pregnancy, or other circumstances affecting practice. Yoga can cause injuries, and beginners should avoid treating extreme positions as a starting point. [3]

For this original introductory session, use a sturdy chair without wheels on a nonslip surface. If standing is part of your practice, place the chair or another solid support within easy reach. Follow existing movement restrictions and skip anything that causes pain, dizziness, or unusual symptoms.

The sequence is a gentle exploration of movement, not the program from the sexual-function trial.

A chair-based session to explore

Supported mountain. Sit comfortably with feet supported and hands resting. Let your trunk feel upright without holding it rigidly. If you prefer standing and it is safe, stand with a secure support available. Take a few ordinary breaths. You do not need to draw in your abdomen or tighten your pelvic floor.

Small seated cat and cow. Remain seated. Make a very small, comfortable movement between slightly rounding and slightly opening your upper trunk. Keep your neck easy and avoid pushing to the end of your spinal range. Try a few slow movements, or skip this if spinal movement is restricted or uncomfortable.

Seated side reach. Keep both feet supported. Rest one hand on the chair. Let the other arm move a short distance outward or upward, only where your shoulder is comfortable, and make a small side bend if appropriate. Return to the middle and consider the other side. There is no need to lift the arm overhead or reach farther each time.

Quiet finish. Return to a supported seat. Let breathing remain natural and notice whether you would like to rest, move, or stop. A brief session is enough to learn whether the format appeals to you.

These options are not suitable for everyone. An instructor with relevant training or a physiotherapist can help adapt the practice more precisely than a general article can.

Keep useful outcomes in view

You might notice that a chair-based session is convenient, that one movement feels pleasant, or that a class with an accommodating teacher helps you keep a routine. Those are worthwhile observations without interpreting them as proof of a sexual-health treatment effect.

If your primary concern is pain during sex, dryness, low desire, or another persistent change, seek an appropriate assessment. Yoga can be discussed alongside care; it should not become a reason to postpone it or blame yourself when symptoms continue.

You do not have to become flexible enough to deserve intimacy. A useful practice gives you more comfortable choices and leaves room for the evidence to remain uncertain.

ONE SMALL THING TO TRY

Choose one comfortable supported movement, respecting existing restrictions and asking for adaptations when needed.
