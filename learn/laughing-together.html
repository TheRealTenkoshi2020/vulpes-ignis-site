Laughing Together: Playfulness as a Form of Intimacy

Humor can build closeness when nobody becomes the punchline.

For adults 18+. Education, not a diagnosis or personalized care.

Shared laughter can help people feel like teammates. The useful distinction is between laughing with someone and laughing at a vulnerability they trusted you with.

Try it together

Make a list of ridiculous low-stakes activities: a kitchen dance-off, inventing dramatic names for household objects, or rating imaginary vacation destinations. Let both people veto an idea.

An everyday example

A couple invents a silly award for whoever finds the best bargain at the grocery store. Their running joke becomes a shared ritual.

Consent and comfort

Avoid pranks involving embarrassment, fear, deception, or sexual boundaries. If a joke falls flat, apologize instead of insisting someone should find it funny. Neither partner owes an explanation for declining or changing their mind.

A conversation starter

What kind of humor makes you feel closer, and what kind makes you withdraw?

Make it your own

Choose one part of the idea that fits your relationship and leave the rest. If you try it, check in afterward: did this feel enjoyable, neutral, or uncomfortable? Curiosity is useful even when the answer is 'not for us.' Respect privacy, accessibility needs, and differences in desire.

Putting the idea into practice

Create a tiny shared comedy tradition

Try naming your weekly chores like dramatic movie titles, inventing an imaginary restaurant review for dinner, or choosing a ridiculous theme for a walk. These are intentionally low-stakes. A running joke can become a reminder that the relationship contains friendship as well as responsibility.

Know the difference between teasing and trust

Humor about someone's body, trauma, family, or insecurities can hurt even if the speaker intends affection. If a joke lands badly, acknowledge it. 'I was only joking' explains intent but does not repair the impact.

One more way to make it work

A useful test for a new joke is whether the person being teased could comfortably ask you to stop. If not, the joke is probably not mutual. Choose humor about shared circumstances rather than vulnerabilities: the stubborn houseplant, the disastrous recipe, or the absurdly long shopping list. Revisiting a funny memory together can be enough; no performance is required.

A deeper dive

Design humor for two audiences

Before a joke, ask whether both people are likely to enjoy it. An inside joke about a harmless mishap can create belonging; a joke about insecurity, trauma, money, or sexual performance can make someone feel exposed. Humor is especially tricky during disagreements because laughter may be interpreted as dismissal. If the timing is wrong, put the joke away.

Build a repeatable ritual

Choose a low-stakes challenge: invent a fictional restaurant menu, narrate the cat's imaginary career, or trade intentionally terrible movie pitches. Give everyone a way to pass. Notice whether the game leaves both people lighter or whether one person is doing all the entertaining. The point is a shared experience, not making your partner laugh on command.
