Sexual-Health Vaccines Beyond HPV: Hepatitis A, Hepatitis B and Mpox

Which vaccine questions belong in my prevention plan?

Source-checked education with original examples and practical questions.

For adults 18+. Education, not a diagnosis or personalized care.

A prevention conversation can include more than condoms and testing. Vaccines have their own role: helping prevent particular infections before exposure. They do not all prevent the same diseases, and a record of one vaccine does not establish protection against another.

HPV vaccination already has its own Vulpes Ignis guide. This article introduces three other useful questions to bring to a clinician or pharmacist: hepatitis A, hepatitis B and mpox, called monkeypox on some official pages.

Begin with records and circumstances

Find any available vaccination record, including dates and product names if known. An incomplete memory is not a reason to guess that you are fully vaccinated or start a series independently. Ask the service how to check records and determine what is needed.

Relevant circumstances can include prior vaccination, age, health conditions, travel, work, household contacts and sexual or other exposure patterns. Recommendations and access can change, so use current official guidance rather than an old screenshot of an eligibility checklist.

Hepatitis A is not only a travel question

Hepatitis A affects the liver and can spread through fecal-oral exposure, including some close or sexual contact. CDC recommends vaccination for various groups and also states that a person who requests vaccination can receive it. A clinician can help determine whether your record and circumstances call for it. [1]

That makes a practical question possible without an identity-based assumption: “Do my vaccination history and possible exposures make hepatitis A vaccination relevant?” Hygiene and avoiding contact when someone is ill may still matter; vaccination does not erase every gastrointestinal-infection concern.

Ask which product and series apply, whether you need a later dose, and where completion will be recorded. A vaccination plan is easier to maintain when the next step is concrete.

Hepatitis B deserves its own review

Hepatitis B is a different virus. It can spread through blood and certain body fluids, including sexual exposure, and can cause chronic liver infection. CDC's vaccination recommendations distinguish age and exposure circumstances. Being vaccinated against hepatitis A does not establish hepatitis B immunity. [2]

Tell the clinician about any previous hepatitis diagnosis, testing or vaccination. Ask whether testing is relevant and what the available vaccine options require. The answers may differ with medical history and product choice.

Do not interpret a partial record on your own as proof that a booster is needed. CDC notes that boosters are often unnecessary; whether further assessment or vaccination is appropriate depends on the situation. [2]

Mpox prevention includes close-contact circumstances

Mpox can spread through close contact, including intimate skin-to-skin contact. Vaccination recommendations address people with particular exposure risks and other defined circumstances, rather than every adult having the same need. A person's gender or orientation alone is not a complete assessment. [3]

CDC's US guidance describes the JYNNEOS series as two doses, generally four weeks apart. If a dose is late, ask how to complete the series rather than assuming it no longer counts. Vaccinated people should still avoid close contact with someone who has mpox and seek assessment for concerning symptoms. [3]

If a relevant exposure has already occurred, ask a healthcare or public-health service promptly about post-exposure options. Do not wait for a routine preventive visit to discuss a known contact.

Separate prevention, symptoms and treatment

A vaccine appointment cannot diagnose a new rash, sore, fever or other symptom. Tell the service about symptoms or recent exposures before arriving so it can advise on the appropriate setting and precautions.

Similarly, an STI test is not a substitute for reviewing vaccination, and a vaccine does not replace all future testing. These measures answer different questions and can sit alongside each other in a practical prevention plan.

Make the next appointment easier

This original Vulpes Ignis checklist has six lines: records I have; records I still need; health or exposure questions; the vaccine and product recommended; the next dose or review date; and costs or access questions.

Ask for a copy of what was given. You do not need to memorize every schedule. You need a reliable record, an understandable reason for the recommendation and a next step you can actually complete.
