The Sunday Connection Check-In

A short weekly ritual for appreciation, plans, boundaries, and repair.

For adults 18+. Read to learn and prepare questions; a qualified professional can help with your own care.

Regular check-ins can help small needs become visible before they turn into resentments.

An idea to try

Set aside fifteen minutes for three questions: What felt good this week? What was difficult? What would help next week? Each person can pass.

A realistic example

Two partners realize they both need more quiet time and plan one screen-free evening.

Consent and comfort

Keep the ritual collaborative, not a weekly performance review. If a serious disagreement arises, make time for it separately.

Talk about it

What one adjustment would make the coming week easier for us?

Make it yours

Choose only what feels enjoyable and manageable. An invitation can be declined or changed at any time, and no one owes physical intimacy in exchange for attention or effort. If you try the idea, ask what worked and what should change. A conversation that leads nowhere else can still be worthwhile.

Putting the idea into practice

A practical fifteen-minute agenda

Start with one appreciation each. Spend five minutes on logistics, five on emotional connection, and five on one small adjustment for the coming week. If a serious issue appears, schedule time to address it rather than rushing through it.

Keep the meeting human

Avoid scoring who contributed more or saving complaints for a weekly ambush. Some weeks the best check-in is a shorter conversation. The goal is staying informed about each other's lives, not creating another performance metric.

One more way to make it work

Keep notes only if both people find them useful, and avoid recording sensitive disclosures without agreement. The purpose is to notice patterns: what improved, what remained difficult, and which practical adjustment might help. If the conversation becomes heated, stop the routine and address the issue properly rather than rushing to finish the agenda.

A deeper dive

A fifteen-minute agenda

Spend a few minutes appreciating what worked, then name one friction point and one manageable adjustment. End by deciding who will do what and when. Avoid trying to resolve every issue in one sitting. A short check-in works best when it feels safe to say 'I need to think about that.'

Prevent the weekly audit

Do not keep score or use the ritual to list a partner's failures. If a topic involves serious conflict, schedule separate time or seek appropriate support. Some weeks a simple 'We're okay, but tired' may be enough. Consistency is useful only when it remains voluntary and constructive.
