Your Body, Your Exam: Choices During Pelvic and Genital Care

What can I ask before and during an intimate examination?

Read at your own pace. Use the questions and examples that fit your life.

For adults 18+. Read to learn and prepare questions; a qualified professional can help with your own care.

An intimate examination can feel routine, unfamiliar, uncomfortable or frightening. You do not have to know the correct medical vocabulary to ask what is happening or explain what would help.

This guide offers preparation questions for adult care. It describes professional expectations and practical requests, rather than guaranteeing that every option or legal rule is identical in every clinic or country.

Ask what the examination would answer

Before undressing, ask why the examination is being proposed, which parts it includes and how the findings could change your care. ACOG distinguishes pelvic examinations performed for symptoms or relevant history from decisions about routine examinations in people without symptoms. A clinic visit does not automatically mean every possible internal examination is needed. [1]

Try: “Are we investigating my symptom, doing a screening test, or both?” Screening and symptom assessment can involve different decisions. Ask whether there are alternatives, what they can and cannot show, and what the consequences of postponing the proposed examination would be.

The aim is an understandable decision, not having to guess which procedure is essential.

Request an explanation before each step

The UK's General Medical Council instructs clinicians to explain the purpose and nature of intimate examinations, address questions and obtain consent. Its guidance also emphasizes dignity, privacy and attention to discomfort. These are professional standards from a particular jurisdiction, not a description of every country's law. [2]

You can ask: “Please explain before touching me,” or “Tell me when the next part begins.” Ask which sensations are expected and which should be reported. If a word is unclear, request plain language.

Unexpected distress is a reason to pause and communicate. It is not a test you have to pass to earn the clinician's patience.

Discuss a chaperone and your own support

GMC guidance says a suitable chaperone should be offered for intimate examinations. A clinical chaperone has a professional role; a friend, partner or support worker may provide different reassurance. Ask the clinic what arrangements are available and whether both can be present when appropriate. [2]

If a particular arrangement matters, contact the service before attending when possible. Ask about clinician preferences, communication assistance or accessibility needs at the same time. Services may need to arrange another appointment, and they should explain the options and any clinical urgency.

You can also request time to speak with the clinician without a companion present.

Make the pause signal explicit

Before the exam, you could say: “If I say pause or raise my hand, please stop and check with me before continuing.” Choose a signal you can use in the position planned for the examination.

GMC guidance requires clinicians to tell patients they can ask for an examination to stop, check continuing consent and stop if asked. Ask your own clinician how they will respond to a pause or withdrawal of consent. [2]

A pause can be used to change position, ask a question or decide against continuing. You do not have to disclose a trauma history to explain that you want more control over the pace.

Ask for adjustments that matter to you

Consider whether you need an interpreter, information in writing, help transferring or positioning, a quieter setting, extra explanation or particular terms for body parts. The clinician can discuss which adjustments are possible and how an alternative approach might affect the examination.

You might say: “I can manage one step at a time,” or “Please let me get dressed before we discuss the results.” If an adjustment is unavailable, ask why and whether another service or appointment arrangement could help. Asking does not require you to apologize for having access needs.

Leave with a result and follow-up plan

Ask what was observed, what remains uncertain and which samples or further tests are involved. Confirm when results should arrive, how they will be sent and whom to contact if you do not hear back. Explain any privacy concerns before messages are sent.

If you want a reminder to bring with you, note the questions you want answered, how you prefer the steps explained, any support you would like, your pause signal and how results should reach you. It is a communication aid, not blanket consent. Each examination still needs a current conversation, with room for your decision to change as you learn more.

ONE SMALL THING TO TRY

Before an intimate examination, ask what it is for and how each step will be explained. Agree on a pause signal and mention any support or adjustments you want to discuss.
