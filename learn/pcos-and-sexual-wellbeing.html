PCOS and Sexual Wellbeing: Symptoms, Mood and Fertility Questions

How can care address my sexual wellbeing without reducing it to weight or fertility?

Read at your own pace. Use the questions and examples that fit your life.

For adults 18+. Read to learn and prepare questions; a qualified professional can help with your own care.

PCOS can enter a conversation through periods, metabolic health, skin or hair changes, fertility or a diagnosis made for another reason. Sexual wellbeing deserves room in that conversation too, without becoming another outcome someone is expected to optimize.

This guide keeps the familiar term PCOS. Monash University's guideline hub now also uses PMOS, or polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome, following its terminology update in 2026. Older research and many people's records still use PCOS; bring the actual history and questions to care rather than relying on a name alone. [1]

Ask permission for the topic, including from yourself

You may want to discuss desire, arousal, comfort, body image, relationships or nothing sexual at all. A diagnosis does not create an obligation to disclose intimate details to every clinician.

The international guideline's psychosexual recommendations emphasize seeking permission to discuss sexual function and considering the person's distress and context. A lower level of interest is not automatically a problem that needs correction simply because someone else expects more sex. [2]

An original opening sentence is: “I would like to include a sexual-health concern in this visit,” or “I am comfortable with my preferences and want help with this separate symptom.”

Consider several contributors without assuming a single cause

The guideline identifies factors that can influence psychosexual wellbeing, including symptoms affecting appearance, mood, infertility concerns and medicines. These are possible contributors to assess, not a description of how every person with the condition feels. [2]

Record which issue matters to you and when it changed. For example, medication timing, pain, fatigue and feeling scrutinized by a partner lead to different questions. A care plan is more useful when it addresses that actual concern rather than treating the diagnosis as an explanation for everything.

New pain, abnormal bleeding or another significant symptom still deserves appropriate assessment. Do not assume that an existing endocrine diagnosis accounts for every later change.

Keep body concerns separate from a weight prescription

If appearance-related symptoms or comments from others affect comfort, say so. You can request care that addresses the symptom and its impact without accepting shame or a requirement to change your body before discussing intimacy.

The international guideline recognizes body-image and psychological concerns and emphasizes individualized, respectful care. Ask what a proposed treatment is meant to improve and whether its evidence fits that goal. [2]

A partner can ask what feels supportive rather than trying to talk someone out of their feelings or grade whether a body “looks healthy.” Practical respect may include privacy, different lighting, a pause or no physical activity.

Give fertility its own place

PCOS does not tell a clinician whether a particular person wants pregnancy, is trying to conceive or needs contraception. State the current goal rather than letting the appointment assume one.

If you are considering pregnancy or have difficulty conceiving, ask how the cycle pattern and medical history affect the assessment. ASRM identifies irregular cycles and known reproductive concerns as reasons evaluation may be appropriate before a generic waiting period. [3]

If pregnancy is not wanted, discuss contraception suited to your needs. An irregular cycle should not be treated as a guarantee that pregnancy cannot happen.

Review treatment in relation to your priorities

Bring all medicines and supplements and note benefits, side effects and difficulties taking them. A treatment may help one aspect of health while leaving a sexual concern unresolved. Ask whether medication review, symptom care or a referral would be useful. [4]

Be cautious with products promising to “balance hormones” or restore a universal sexual response. Ask what was studied, in whom and for which outcome before spending money or changing treatment.

Prepare a goals-first appointment note

Before an appointment, make a short note of the concern, its impact, current treatment, reproductive goal if relevant, and what respectful care would look like. Finish with: “Which next step fits my priority, and when will we review it?”

You deserve care for the life you are actually living, including the freedom to decide which intimate questions matter to you.

ONE SMALL THING TO TRY

Choose your priority for the appointment: comfort, mood, a symptom, a medicine question or a reproductive goal. Ask for a next step that addresses what matters to you.
